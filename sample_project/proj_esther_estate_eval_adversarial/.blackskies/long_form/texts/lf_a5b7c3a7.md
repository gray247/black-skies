The thirteenth chime still echoed in her ears, a relentless reminder that time was slipping through her fingers like sand, each grain heavy with memories best left undisturbed.

Jun shifted beside her, the faint smell of damp earth and wet wool rising from his soaked coat. He looked at her, eyes wide and glassy, the lantern in his hand sputtering weakly, casting flickering shapes on the walls that seemed to dance mockingly at their fear. “I didn’t hear anything,” he said, but the tremor in his voice suggested otherwise, an undercurrent of doubt threading through his resolve.

The silence of the nursery loomed, oppressive like a storm cloud ready to spill its rain. Clara could almost feel the walls pressing in, the house itself holding its breath, waiting for their next move. She glanced down at the chipped ceramic fox nestled in her coat pocket, its painted eyes glimmering faintly in the dim light, a relic of childhood innocence that now felt burdened by the weight of expectation. It had been a gift once, a token of comfort, but now it seemed like a talisman of something darker, something they could not name.

“Maybe we should go back,” Jun suggested, casting a glance over his shoulder, as if he could sense the unseen presence lurking behind them, waiting to slip through the cracks of the old house. Clara’s heart sank at the thought, a tight knot of anxiety twisting in her chest. Back to Nora’s forced cheerfulness and the dusty dining room? Back to the uncertainty of what lay ahead?

“No,” she said, her voice firmer than she felt. “We can’t. Not now.” She had to know. The cough—the way it echoed in her mind, a child’s laugh turned to a sigh—was it a specter of her own memories or something more sinister? The chain rattled again from the service corridor, an insistent reminder that they were not alone; something else was here, too, testing their resolve, trying to pull them back into the shadows.

“Clara,” Jun’s voice was low, almost pleading. “What if it’s just—”

“What if it’s not?” Clara interrupted, the edge of her fear sharpening her tone. She could feel the weight of his gaze, the concern etched into his features, but her resolve stiffened like the cold air around them. She reached for the key tied to her wrist, fingers brushing against the red ribbon, a lifeline that felt both reassuring and suffocating.

The lantern flickered again, a final gasp of light, and she leaned closer to the door, her breath hitching as she pressed her ear against the wood. For a heartbeat, the world fell away, leaving only the thrum of her pulse and the weight of the house, alive and watchful. She imagined—no, she felt—a soft rustle from within, the sound of something shifting in the darkness. The cough, a small, innocent thing, echoed again in her mind, and she could almost see it, the source of that sound waiting just beyond the threshold, lost and afraid.

“Let’s just try it,” she said, her voice barely above a whisper, but it felt like a challenge thrown into the silence. With a deep breath, she turned the key in the lock, the ancient mechanism groaning in protest as if the house were trying to keep its secrets buried beneath layers of dust and memory.
