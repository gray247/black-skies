Clara pressed her back against the cold, wood-paneled wall, her heart thrumming a frantic rhythm as she fidgeted with the red ribbon tied around her wrist. The fabric felt slick against her skin, a reminder of the greenhouse’s damp embrace, where they had first unearthed the parlor key from the grasp of wet vines. Yet now, tangled in her thoughts, she couldn’t recall the last time she’d felt anything but disquiet.

Jun's coat clung to him, heavy with the remnants of the rain, each droplet that slid down his collar a reminder of the storm still brewing outside. He shivered, the chill creeping past the fabric and into his bones. “You think we should knock?” His voice cut through the silence, a thin veneer of bravado barely masking the uncertainty beneath.

Clara’s gaze lingered on the door, the wood worn and weary, as if it had borne witness to too many secrets, too many fears. “What if someone’s in there?” she whispered, a tremor threading through her words. The idea seemed to hang in the air, heavy and tangible, as if the house itself had ears.

Jun shifted, the lantern sputtering again, casting warped shadows that danced along the ornate wallpaper, twisting the patterns into grotesque forms. “There’s no one here, Clara. Nora said…” He faltered, the words caught in his throat. The way Nora’s eyes had flitted away when Clara had shown her the ceramic fox, how she had flinched, sent a shiver of doubt through Clara, pulling her deeper into the shadows of her own thoughts.

The chain rattled once more from the corridor, a dry, metallic clink that echoed ominously in the stillness. Clara's breath hitched. “Did you hear that?” Her voice was barely a whisper, almost lost in the creaks of the house settling around them. She turned to Jun, searching his face for something—reassurance, perhaps, or a shared acknowledgment of the tension that had curled itself around them like smoke.

He nodded slowly, the lantern dimming further, its glow reduced to a mere suggestion of light. “It’s probably just the wind,” he said, but there was a tremor in his voice that betrayed him. “Or the house… it does that.” His eyes darted toward the door, as if expecting it to burst open at any moment, revealing whatever lurked behind it.

But Clara didn’t think it was the wind. She felt it in her bones, a creeping dread that the house was alive in ways they could not comprehend. The muddy prints leading them here were fresh, unmistakable, and they hadn’t come from either of them. “What if it’s a child?” she murmured, the thought slipping from her lips before she could catch it.

Jun’s brow furrowed. “There hasn’t been a child in this house for years, Clara. You know that.” Yet his gaze lingered on the door, as if he, too, could hear the phantom cough she had not.

“I heard it,” she insisted, the anger rising within her, a deflection against the fear that threatened to consume her. “We need to open the door. We can’t just stand here.”
